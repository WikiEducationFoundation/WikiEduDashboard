Check: source reliability and independence (WP:RS, WP:INDEPENDENT, WP:PRIMARY, WP:MEDRS).

Assess every distinct source the article cites. Fill the `sources` list with one entry per source, in the order the sources first appear:
- source: the work and publisher or outlet as briefly as possible, such as "The Guardian" or "Smith 2019, Journal of Ecology".
- citation: the reference name or a short excerpt of the citation, copied from the article, so the author can find it.
- source_type: "primary" (original research, the subject's own statements, raw data, court records, press releases), "secondary" (analysis or reporting by someone else), "tertiary" (encyclopedias, textbooks summarizing a field), or "unclear".
- reliability: "generally_reliable", "use_with_caution" (acceptable only for some claims, such as an opinion piece or a self-published expert), "unreliable" (user-generated content, tabloids, content farms, predatory journals, AI-generated sites), or "unclear" when the citation doesn't give enough to judge. Judge by English Wikipedia's usual standards for that kind of source; where the community has a well-known position on a specific outlet, follow it.
- independent: "no" when the source is produced by or for the subject (its own website, press releases, sponsored content, interviews with the subject, work by the subject's employer or funder), "yes" when it is clearly independent, "unclear" otherwise.
- notes: one short sentence explaining any rating other than generally_reliable and independent, or an empty string.

Then report findings for the problems the table shows: content that rests mainly on unreliable or non-independent sources, primary sources used for interpretation or analysis, notability resting on sources that aren't independent, and, for medical content, sources that aren't secondary reviews or guidelines (WP:MEDRS). Quote the citation in each finding. Don't report formatting problems or missing citations; other checks cover those.
